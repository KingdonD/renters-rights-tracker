Policy Implementation Tracker

Renters’ Rights Act

Tracking the phased rollout of the UK’s Renters’ Rights Act

Current status

Phase 1 is live.

Since 1 May 2026, the new tenancy regime is in force. Section 21 “no fault” evictions have been abolished. Fixed-term assured shorthold tenancies have been replaced by rolling periodic tenancies with no end date. Rent can be increased only once a year, through a formal Section 13 notice. Phases 2 and 3, which build the enforcement and standards architecture, are still to come.

Phase Timeline

  1. Phase 1 — The new tenancy system

    Complete

    Live since 1 May 2026

    • Section 21 abolished.
    • All assured shorthold tenancies, including existing ones, converted to assured periodic tenancies with no fixed term.
    • Landlords must now use Section 8 grounds to seek possession.
    • Rent increases limited to once per year via a Section 13 notice.
    • A ban on demanding more than one month’s rent up front.
    • New rules against discriminating against tenants with children or on benefits.

  2. Phase 2 — The enforcement infrastructure

    Upcoming

    Rollout begins late 2026

    • The Private Rented Sector Database, a register landlords must sign up to, begins a regional rollout from late 2026, with fuller rollout expected in 2027.
    • The PRS Landlord Ombudsman, a redress scheme landlords will be required to join, follows after the database, and the government expects it to be operational in 2028.

    This is the phase to watch most closely, because it is where the Act either gains teeth or slips.

  3. Phase 3 — Raising standards

    Upcoming

    Dates mostly unconfirmed, running to 2030 and beyond

    • Private landlords will need to meet an energy performance rating of EPC C, or hold a valid exemption, by 1 October 2030.
    • The Decent Homes Standard will be extended to the private rented sector.
    • The government has committed to consult on extending Awaab’s Law, the duty to fix hazards within set timescales, to private renting.

    Most dates here are still to be confirmed through secondary legislation.

Stakeholder Views

Generation Rent (tenant campaign group)

Has welcomed the Act as historic and celebrated the end of Section 21, but argues the reforms will only work if enforcement is properly resourced. Its chief executive gave evidence to the London Assembly Housing Committee in January 2026 warning that councils need stronger capacity to hold landlords accountable. The group is also campaigning hard on what it calls the biggest gap in the law, the absence of rent controls.

generationrent.org

Shelter (housing and homelessness charity)

Has campaigned for these reforms for years and frames the Act as a major win for renters, with particular emphasis on the end of Section 21 and stronger protections for groups who have faced discrimination in the rental market. Watches the rollout of the database and ombudsman as the test of whether the rights become real in practice.

england.shelter.org.uk

National Residential Landlords Association (landlord body)

Represents landlords and has focused on the practical burden of compliance: the transition of existing tenancies, the new possession grounds, and the coming requirement to register on the database. Its public material is largely operational guidance for members preparing for each phase, which itself signals how significant the changes are for the sector.

nrla.org.uk

House of Commons Library (neutral parliamentary research service)

Provides the impartial reference point. Its briefing sets out the three-phase implementation plan, the scale of the private rented sector, and the secondary legislation still required, without taking a side. Useful as the plain-facts anchor against the advocacy voices above.
